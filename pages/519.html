Arc 7 Chapter 21: "Zikr Osman"

—Vollachia Empire's General of the Second Unit, Zikr Osman, is known as a "Womanizer."

There are various words used to describe him — a lecher, a philanderer, a devotee of carnal love.
For an Imperial soldier who must demonstrate his ability and win the respect of officers and men, this is quite a dishonorable string of names to be saddled with.
And yet, Zikr Osman himself was fond of this title of "Womanizer."
—No, it would not be an exaggeration to say he took pride in it.

Why was that?
It was because, before he was called "Womanizer," the nickname that had been attached to Zikr was one he found unbearably distasteful.
As a soldier, being called by that nickname was an intolerable humiliation. And so he proudly bore the title of "Womanizer," which had made his officers and men forget that other name.

To begin with, even calling it "a liking for women," what Zikr had was of a different character from the sort of male bad habit commonly meant by the term — being a skirt-chaser, or looking down on women.

The Osman family was a lineage that had produced Imperial soldiers for generations, but in Zikr's generation the blood ran strangely lopsided, and for some reason his family ended up consisting of nothing but women. Born and raised in a household with four older sisters and six younger sisters, Zikr spent his childhood without a single brother.
Raised surrounded by sisters both older and younger, Zikr — like most people in such circumstances — had various preconceptions about women instilled in him.
The difference between him and most people, though, was this: while most people whose families are full of women lose their illusions about women as a result, Zikr instead came to seek ideals and illusions in the women outside his family.

And then, parting from the sisters who grieved to let go of him — their beloved little brother, their gentle older brother — he set out young on the path of an Imperial soldier, and in his first encounters with women outside his family, something in him burst open.

From then on, to Zikr Osman, women became something like a fleeting dream existing in the gap between ideal and reality — a forbidden fruit in which love and obsession intermingled.

Having grown up jostled among a great many female family members, Zikr was gentle toward women, and at the same time held a strong desire to be treated gently by women in turn. And so he devoted himself to women, and considered being devoted to by women the highest joy — and put both into practice.
This attitude, so different from that of the many domineering men of the Empire, combined with the jealousy and contempt directed at him for his precise generalship and his string of unremarkable-but-solid victories, and thus led to the name "Womanizer."

But as told at the outset, Zikr Osman was fond of that epithet.
"Womanizer" — well, and why not? To begin with, there were more men who liked women than men who disliked them, so it gave him occasion to converse with a great many officers and men.
Having settled the matter in his own mind this way, Zikr's bearing was without hesitation, and his subordinates, knowing their superior's tastes, naturally came to respect him as the man hailed as "Womanizer."

—"Womanizer." Well, and why not?

Even while called that, the man who had risen to the rank of Imperial General of the Second Unit was none other than Zikr Osman.
When the Emperor was driven from his throne, and his political enemies, working within the bounds of authority permitted them, sought to finish him off, the best move they could find was none other than him.

That was Zikr Osman — the mediocre general of a threat, dismissed with the mere epithet "Womanizer."

From the very start, Zikr had sensed that this expedition carried a whiff of something suspicious.

Imperial Army maneuvers were held every year in the area surrounding the Buddheim Jungle in the eastern part of the Empire, but this year the timing had been moved up, and the true purpose of the expedition — negotiations with the "People of Shudrak" — had been disclosed only to a select few officers, Zikr among them.

"The 'People of Shudrak,' hidden away in Buddheim…"

They were an indigenous people living in the Buddheim Jungle, a tribe known to have an ancient history.
They were also famous for rarely emerging from the jungle, and Zikr himself had never encountered them directly. Still, he had heard they were a matrilineal tribe, and he would have liked the chance to meet one, just once.
The idea that they lived in the jungle, capturing outside men and using them as tools to bear children, was intriguing too. Presumably, they were all strong, sturdy women.

Women were fine things. Strong or weak, kind or severe, each had its own flavor.
To begin with, they weren't something that could be lumped into one category. Even a single sister could be kind at times and severe at others, sweet at times and bitter at others.
If one woman alone could show such shape-shifting splendor, how could anyone possibly lump multiple women together as one thing?
And so—

"Force them to swear fealty, or else annihilate them… what is the capital thinking?"

That was the secret order handed down to Zikr before the expedition set out.
From the capital, Lupugana, it had been declared outright that the true purpose of this expedition was either to win over the "People of Shudrak," or else to destroy them. He had confirmed whether this might be some mistake, but the capital's answer did not change, and Zikr had no choice but to accept it.

Still, he had heard word that some sort of trouble was brewing in the capital as well, and he could well imagine that it was connected to the true purpose of this expedition.
That much was clear from the fact that he had been ordered to keep the expedition's purpose hidden, concealed even from his own officers and men, as he carried it out. —What His Imperial Majesty was thinking, Zikr could not say.

"Though, I suppose no one can read the depths of that man's mind."

—Vincent Vollachia, seventy-seventh Emperor of the Sacred Vollachia Empire.

That was the name of the man who stood at the summit ruling this Empire, the foremost sage of the present age, one who never let others glimpse the depths of his thoughts.
The Emperor, said to see through this vast Empire in its entirety from the Crystal Palace in the capital, Lupugana — even holding the rank of General of the Second Unit, Zikr, entrusted with a post far from the capital, had few chances to look upon His Majesty's face. But his wisdom and renown had reached Zikr's ears across the distance regardless.

Imperialism holds it inevitable that the strong devour the weak and rise ever hungrily upward.
In a world where, day to day, various tribes rose up in rebellion here and there and the sparks of civil strife caught flame, Vincent had crushed every problem before it could grow into a great conflagration.
In the eight years since his reign began, the Vollachia Empire had enjoyed a startling peace.

Blood still spilled, fires still rose, lives were still lost.
Even so, this present age was the most peaceful era the Vollachia Empire had known since its founding.

Precisely because of that, the thoroughgoing hardline stance toward the "People of Shudrak" carved a furrow into Zikr's brow.
Though he was a child born of war, His Majesty's dispositions seemed somehow to shun war itself. Zikr had privately imagined that this was not because His Majesty abhorred war, but because he judged war to be a worthless thing.
Was it because he felt that judgment betrayed now that this feeling arose in him?

"No, that's not true at all, General Zikr. I understand His Majesty's thinking well enough. Even from my position as a mere foot soldier, I have my own thoughts on the matter."

So said one common soldier, listening with an easy, likable smile.
This was at a tavern in the walled city of Guaral, where the expeditionary force was garrisoned as its base of operations. On nights when he did not spend his time with a woman, Zikr preferred to drink with his subordinates like this — and moreover, to drink not with the senior officers or Generals of the Third Unit who attended him, but with common soldiers of much lower rank.

Of course, most soldiers would rather not go out of their way to drink with a superior officer.
Even so, Zikr favored this kind of ritual on every expedition, wanting to grasp the thoughts and inclinations of the subordinates accompanying him. —Though this particular night, he may have talked a bit too much.

The common soldier he drank with that day had an unusually glib tongue, and was a lively conversationalist.
As he drank, the man kept glancing curiously about, and when asked what he was thinking, he joked that he was working out, just in case, how he'd handle it if this very tavern suddenly became a battlefield.
That readiness for battle at all times, combined with his fearless attitude toward a superior officer, struck a chord of goodwill in Zikr both for the man's Imperial spirit and his personal character — and Zikr ended up saying things he ought not to have said.

Even the secret order regarding the "People of Shudrak" — though he never stated it outright — Zikr had the distinct feeling he'd been drawn into confirming it all the same.
Had this been some agent of a foreign power, it would have been a blunder deserving the harshest punishment.

"There's no need to worry, sir. I'll be heading to the front lines myself as early as tomorrow, on His Majesty's orders… well, I'm sure the higher-ups have all sorts of schemes in mind, but that's none of my concern."

The common soldier said this as though to reassure Zikr, whose intoxication was fading and who was coming back to his senses.
And true to his word, the man departed for the encampment that would serve as the expedition's front line, the camp adjoining the Buddheim Jungle.

Having confirmed this, and remaining behind in the city, Zikr once again faced the knot in his chest.
The thoroughgoing hardline stance toward the "People of Shudrak," the capital's order demanding submission or death — even so, Zikr resolved to hold, as far as possible, to a posture of persuasion.
He believed that this was closer to what he trusted was His Majesty's true conviction.
But then—

"—The camp was burned down in a Shudrak raid, you say?"

Receiving this unexpected report, Zikr was left dumbfounded in the requisitioned city hall.
Just the night before, he had been thinking of extending every possible accommodation to the "People of Shudrak," of drawing their tribe in through means other than warfare.

Multiple encampments deployed to encircle the western side of the Buddheim Jungle had come under attack from the "People of Shudrak," and the Imperial soldiers, unable even to mount a counterattack, had been routed.
Shot in the back as they fled, the casualties had mounted further, until more than a hundred had been lost.

"Impossible…"

Whether that murmur was directed at his own judgment, at the actions of the "People of Shudrak," or at reality itself, even Zikr himself could not say.
But one thing was certain: the peaceable plan he had been building in his mind had collapsed, and the "People of Shudrak" had become enemies — not merely of his own Imperial soldiers, but of His Majesty the Emperor.

"It's a shameful thing to admit, but we will await reinforcements from the capital, and then strike down the rebels of Buddheim Jungle."

Having taken in the soldiers who had barely escaped the Shudrak raid with their lives, and having reorganized what remained into the semblance of an expeditionary force, Zikr made that decision.
There had been the option of pressing into the forest with his current strength and engaging the "People of Shudrak" directly. But the jungle was their domain, and any half-measure numerical advantage might well be erased there.
To secure certain victory, what was needed was not a half-measure advantage, but an overwhelming one.

And soldiers, moreover, who had cast off any soft posture of seeking negotiation — elite troops meant purely for annihilation.

"Foolishly, it was they who cast aside the hand offered for peace. Very well — then in loyalty to His Majesty, we have no choice but to mete out punishment upon the rebels."

Having thus steeled himself, even from "Womanizer" Zikr Osman, all mercy vanished.
Even if the enemy was the matrilineal "People of Shudrak," he would have to strike down every last one of their bloodline, down to the last member, and cut off any cause for future regret.
To that end—

"Do not neglect the preparations to seal the main gate. I hear the 'People of Shudrak' are skilled with the bow, but they cannot scale the walls of this walled city. Leave no room through which they might break through."

Having drawn from the returning soldiers the circumstances under which the front-line encampment had been burned, and bearing in mind that it had been the work of a small elite raiding party, Zikr shifted entirely toward a defensive strategy.
Given what was known of the "People of Shudrak's" way of life, it seemed unlikely that the tribe's total numbers were especially large, and it seemed doubtful that everyone — including children and the elderly — could fight.
At most, if their fighting strength were estimated at around a hundred, then for them to contend with Imperial soldiers, their only real option would be some sort of surprise attack under cover of night.
But even that was a means of preemptive attack that only worked against an opponent whose vigilance had slackened for lack of any attack.

"Seal every gap, thoroughly! Even a walled city's walls are not impregnable. Given a long enough history, one can well imagine there being ways to reach the outside without passing through the gates. Do not overlook anything resembling a hidden passage!"

"On that point, a report for you, sir. Some of the soldiers who returned from the burned encampment have already been going around sealing off hidden passages in preparation against attack from outside."

"Is that so? It's heartening to know there are those with foresight even among the ranks below 'General.' Once this business is settled, we shall see about promoting them properly. But for now…"

"—Yes, sir. A thoroughgoing defensive posture until reinforcements arrive, is that correct?"

Hearing Zikr's instructions, his subordinate, a General of the Third Unit, bowed deeply at the waist.
Were this the sort of thing said by an ordinary "General," his passive stance might well have been mocked. Indeed, Zikr himself had once been subjected to that sort of derision.
But Zikr had already suffered a painful blow at the hands of the "People of Shudrak," and were he to return to the capital, he could not escape some manner of punishment.
His back was already against the wall — and in such a situation, there was no reason not to take the best possible move.
His subordinates understood this too, and so did not mock Zikr's stance.
And then—

"…What did you say?"

Holding out under siege in the walled city of Guaral, Zikr raised an eyebrow at his subordinate's report.
In the midst of days spent waiting for reinforcements from the capital while tension crept steadily higher, this particular report brought Zikr a sense of thoroughly misplaced sentiment.
For it was—

"Yes, sir. It seems that over the past few days, a troupe of traveling performers has become quite the talk of the city."

"Traveling performers…"

The report his subordinate had brought was of a startlingly leisurely, pastoral nature.
It was a report ill-suited to the tense wartime circumstances — and yet, there was no reason to reprimand the subordinate for bringing it.
To begin with, it was Zikr himself who had disliked forcing the citizens to live under any particular awareness of being at war.
In a city where an army was garrisoned, discontent tended to accumulate easily enough as it was. Depending on circumstances, failure to control the populace's sentiment could even lead to collapse.

With that in mind, Zikr had deliberately refrained from imposing any strong clampdown on public sentiment.
He maintained constant vigilance toward the outside of the city, and had his soldiers thoroughly search to prevent any infiltration by the "People of Shudrak," while still allowing the citizens to carry on their lives unchanged.
He was well aware of the contradiction in this, but it was the compromise between Zikr's own conscience and his soldierly sense of duty.

In any case, because this policy was in place, the daytime traffic in and out of the city — the checkpoint at the main gate, the treatment of traveling merchants — remained unchanged.
And so there was room for a troupe of traveling performers to have made its way into the city, presumably—

"…And what do you intend by reporting this to me? If you mean for me to crack down on them, I'd rather not. Given the circumstances, I understand the citizens' desire to welcome such a troupe."

No small number of soldiers were out, alongside the city guard, continuing to patrol the city.
Many of the soldiers who had fled the raid and entered the city harbored a growing hostility and wariness toward the "People of Shudrak," and despite instructions to exercise caution, disputes with the citizenry were unending.
Amid all that, it wasn't hard to imagine that a troupe of traveling performers appearing in the city would offer the citizens a modest measure of comfort. And if that were taken from them, moreover—

"The citizens' resentment would explode. You understand that much, surely."

"Of course, sir, just as you say. That's why I'm not telling you to crack down on them. It's only that…"

"Only that, what? You're being rather coy about this."

At his subordinate's hesitant manner, Zikr raised one eyebrow, urging him to continue.
After a moment's silence, the subordinate bowed his head as though resigned to it. Fist to palm, in a posture showing both respect for his superior and the absence of any hostile intent, the subordinate let out a breath and said,

"The truth is, sir, that troupe… the dancer is quite something to behold. What would you say, sir — perhaps you might see her for yourself, just once?"

"Me? A dancer, is it… not entirely without appeal, but—"

Hearing his subordinate's unexpected suggestion, Zikr's eyes widened in surprise.
This was a subordinate with whom he had a long association, who had walked through their share of battlefields together. It seemed unlikely that he would bring up such a proposal without any thought behind it.
And yet Zikr could not fathom the true intent behind his wanting to show him a dancer.

"Sir, I can't say this too loudly, but discontent is rising even among the soldiers."

"Hm…"

At Zikr's questioning look, the subordinate, straightening his posture, confided this.
At the not-so-gentle turn the conversation had taken, Zikr's gaze sharpened naturally. When he silently prompted him to continue, the subordinate lowered his voice slightly and said,

"It isn't only the capital's reluctance to send reinforcements — the men have their own thoughts, it seems, about you holing up in the city and maintaining a defensive posture, sir. There's also the matter of the camp that was burned."

"…I see. No, well, that's only to be expected."

At his subordinate's frank observation, Zikr felt something heavy settle in his chest.
It was only natural that the men's discontent, their distrust of Zikr, should accumulate under the circumstances. Allowing the "People of Shudrak" to strike first, losing so many soldiers — that had been Zikr's own failing.
And since then, the soldiers who had rejoined him had been given no chance to redeem themselves, so it was no wonder if they turned the point of their discontent toward Zikr.

"Among the more loose-tongued of them, sir, you're being called—"

"—Don't say it."

"—! My apologies, sir."

Cutting off his subordinate's words before he could finish, Zikr put a hand to his forehead.
Given the discouraging state of things, he could well imagine what sort of abuse men nursing grievances might hurl at a superior officer. And yet the thing he found hard to bear was precisely those all-too-easy-to-imagine words of insult.
Even having attained the rank of General of the Second Unit, that particular form of abuse was simply something he could not stomach.
And having thought that far, Zikr grasped the intent behind his subordinate's proposal.

"I see. In other words, you're saying I should provide the men an outlet to relieve their discontent."

"Yes, sir. And I thought the dancers might serve that purpose. If they were to see that splendid song and dance, many of the officers and men would surely…"

"Ho, is that so? That's a remarkably vivid description — almost as if you'd seen it yourself?"

At Zikr's narrow-eyed pressing, the subordinate cleared his throat and avoided a direct answer. But that very reaction was proof enough that the suspicion was true.
In any case, being caught between "General" and "men" was his subordinate's difficult position.
To seek some means of soothing that strain was hardly something to be scolded for.
And on top of that, here was a subordinate who had actually seen this dancer for himself, making such an earnest proposal.

"This dancer, it would seem, is quite remarkably beautiful."

"That she is, sir! Ah, no — I'm certain she would meet with your approval as well, sir. And the dance itself, and the musicians' singing and playing, are quite splendid…"

"Hmhm, well, that does raise my expectations somewhat."

Even as he responded so, Zikr privately thought the description was rather exaggerated.
Still, the fact that his subordinate had made such a proposal out of concern for his position and his future prospects was itself a welcome thing, and there was no reason to stubbornly refuse.
Zikr himself, what with the recent busyness and mental strain, had not even kept a woman at his bedside of late.
Nor could he very well impose that same deprivation upon his men.

"Very well, understood. I'll let myself be talked into this, just this once. Invite this troupe of traveling performers, and arrange an occasion to reward the officers and men."

Only—

"—Don't forget to make sure they aren't carrying any weapons in."

Once Zikr had given his permission, his subordinates moved quickly.
Just how starved and parched for such a thing had they been? In no time they had set up a banquet on the first floor of the city hall, prepared wine and food, and gathered women to serve.
Then they sent word to the troupe of traveling performers in question, and welcomed them up to the top floor of the city hall.

"—What you are now about to behold is a lovely dancing maiden, come from beyond the Great Waterfall. With glossy black hair that swallows the light of the sun, beautiful white skin blessed by the Spirits, and a supreme beauty that might make even a heavenly being envious — tonight, she shall dance for you in full splendor."

The musician's grandiose introduction rang out, and slowly, with great ceremony, the veil was drawn back.
A troupe of women performers alone — what the musicians had kept concealed beneath the spread veil was the dancer who had become the talk of the city. As her form was revealed, Zikr's eyes went wide.

"—————"

Baring her white skin, her long black hair flowing loosely down her back, her beauty betrayed the musician's introduction entirely — that is, those words were far too inadequate to express this beauty.
Her beautiful black hair, her white skin wrapped in thin fabric — these certainly possessed a bewitching power to captivate most who beheld them. But these factors were merely one part of the truth of her beauty.
If the charisma that captivated so many — not just dancers and musicians, but people in general — could be likened to a talent, then the atmosphere and bearing that dancer possessed held a frightening abundance of it.

What impressed this upon Zikr most strongly of all, however, was none other than the dancer's eyes.

Long-lashed and almond-shaped, they were not merely at the center of a well-formed countenance — it seemed no exaggeration to say they occupied the single finest point of every golden ratio there was.
That he found himself unconsciously feeling his throat go dry was because his very instincts thirsted for her.

Whether that was because of the dancer herself, or because he was drawn in by the charisma she wore about her, was a phenomenon entirely beyond Zikr's understanding.
Still, if a dancer who had brought such profound emotion even without dancing were to actually begin dancing amid song and music, he could not imagine what manner of shock that would bring.
And Zikr thirsted for that very shock he could not imagine — thirsted to be overwhelmed by it.

"—If it would please you, allow us the opportunity to present our dancing maiden's dance."

In place of the silent dancer — no, dancing maiden — another musician, likewise with long black hair, bowed her head.
The two musicians, one dark-haired and one golden-haired, were beautiful in their own right, but to Zikr, enraptured by the sight of the dancing maiden, they registered as no more than an accompaniment. Ordinarily he would have chided himself for such a dismissive thought toward women, but he was too feverish even for that thought to surface.

Never before had he been so keenly conscious of his epithet, "Womanizer."
In truth, Zikr must indeed have been a "Womanizer" — for before a beauty such as this, it was utterly impossible to keep one's composure.

And so, in that fevered state of mind, the banquet began.
Naturally, as commander, Zikr's seat was at the very back of the hall, where the gathered "Generals" — those of rank above the common soldiers — began to enjoy food and drink.
But the true centerpiece of the occasion was, of course, the traveling troupe's performance.

Though the original purpose had been to reward the officers and men and let them vent their discontent, Zikr forgot that original aim entirely, holding his breath as the dancing maiden's turn came.
He brought wine to his lips to quench his thirst, moistening tongue and lips and throat, and recovered his breath.
That was how thoroughly Zikr's heart had been stolen away.

"—What we present to you tonight is a dance from our dancing maiden's homeland, from beyond the Great Waterfall. A dance come from the farthest of the far — please, savor it to your heart's content."

The musician's voice rose in song, and the plucking of strings wove itself into a slow, gentle melody.
As the unfamiliar tune began to play, even the "Generals" who had until then been chattering noisily fell silent, their flushed faces holding their breath, unwilling to look away from the dance about to begin.
And then—

"—————"

And then, the beautiful "dance" of the slowly emerging dancing maiden began.

"—————"

Making full use of her long limbs, swaying her black hair as she danced — everyone lost the power of speech at the sight.
They forgot to breathe, and simply stared, transfixed. —No, "captivated" is the only word for it. To behold a dance such as this and remain in possession of one's senses would be somehow abnormal.

That would be nothing more than the sensibility of a beast, unable to comprehend the worth of the dance.
Imperial soldiers might be a pack of wolves, but they were not beasts devoid of intellect or language.
And so the "Generals," too, held their breath, forgot to breathe, and were captivated by the beautiful dancing maiden's dance.

Every voice was lost, every eye enraptured by the dancing maiden's dance.
They were captivated by her raven-black hair, by her white skin smooth without a single blemish, by that face of such beautiful, demonic construction that it might make an artist wish to sever his own dominant hand.
But what drew and held Zikr's gaze, his consciousness, his very heart, without fail, was none of these things.

—It was her eyes.

Once again, he found he could not tear his eyes away from the dancer's eyes.
Those almond-shaped eyes surveyed the stage on which she danced, and gazed toward Zikr at the very back of the hall.
It was those eyes, never once straying even for a moment, that reached directly into Zikr's very brain and would not let go.

Presently, the dancing maiden slowly crossed the length of the hall, advancing to stand before Zikr.
And there, she knelt gently, and holding out both hands, asked for his sword.

Naturally, without needing to be told, Zikr understood this as a gesture requesting his blade.
The dancing maiden's dance grew hotter, deeper in color, seizing hold of the world as it moved into its next stage — transitioning into a sword dance, and for that, the dancing maiden desired a sword.

Refusing her was never an option for Zikr.
No one could have stopped it. Neither his subordinates nor the assembled "Generals" — no one could have interfered with that act.
So natural did it seem, as though this were a thing predestined.

And so—

"—You have lost, Zikr Osman."

Even as he heard those cold words declared, the drawn sword pressed against his throat, Zikr Osman still could not comprehend that he had been defeated precisely because he was a "Womanizer."

"—————"

Even in that moment, he could not tear his eyes from the dancing maiden's eyes as she spoke those words.
That cold, overwhelmingly captivating charisma — some sense of having seen it somewhere before continued to burn endlessly through the defeated general Zikr's mind.
