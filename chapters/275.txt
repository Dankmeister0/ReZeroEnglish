Arc 4 Chapter 106: "Otto Suwen"

Grimacing at the tingling, scorched sensation on his skin, Garfiel violently kicked up the drifted fallen leaves.

"Well, look at you go, ya bastard."

The words were muttered irritably, but there was honest admiration hidden in them. Underestimating his opponent had been the cause of his defeat — that was what the man had declared, and it was an undeniable fact.

Garfiel had, without a doubt, completely written Otto off as having no combat ability whatsoever.

"Fire magic stone... the hell was he thinkin', not usin' one with real punch to it?"

A curtain of flame had blotted out his vision for an instant. Garfiel recalled with irritation how it had scorched his frozen body for a mere heartbeat before vanishing. It was practically a bluff of an attack — there was a faint stinging sensation on his skin, but it was no worse than sunburn, nowhere near actual damage.

But one thing was clear.

"If that'd been a shot with real killin' power, even I wouldn'ta walked away from it clean...!"

What should have been a fatal instant had been deliberately turned aside by the other man's judgment. What else could you call this but being shown mercy? He'd been played for a fool by someone who'd pulled his punch and failed to knock him out. It made Garfiel feel pathetic and stupid, plain and simple.

"The hell's he playin' at...!"

What irritated him even more was that the man who'd just attacked him hadn't bothered to press the advantage while Garfiel was distracted by the flames — he'd chosen to flee instantly instead. The clean, decisive move, with not a shred of thought given to a follow-up attack, left Garfiel's reaction lagging, and he completely lost sight of Otto.

Soft earth. Drifts of fallen leaves. Otto had run through unfamiliar terrain with surprising skill — it made sense now, given he'd claimed to have wandered the forest at night. Still, in a straightforward chase, there was no way he could outrun Garfiel. Ten of Otto's strides, Garfiel could close in two. The gap in physical ability between their races was that vast.

And yet Otto had covered even that gap with sheer cunning.

"—Gh! Wh— the hell!? This— aah! Damn it, my nose is done for!"

The instant Garfiel sniffed the air to chase after the fleeing Otto, a stinging odor sharp enough to hurt pierced his nostrils. Having breathed it in fully, Garfiel reeled his head back, shaking it against a pain so sharp his vision flickered.

Looking down, he saw a clear glass bottle lying discarded where Otto had been standing moments before. A colorless liquid was leaking from its open lid, and the pungent smell was clearly wafting from it. But that was as far as his sense of smell could take him now.

"That bastard... don't tell me he thinks sealin' off my nose means he's won."

Baring his fangs, Garfiel let his fury show as, one by one, his means of pursuit were sealed off. Just how long had this man been plotting countermeasures against him? Every single tactic so far had thoroughly and completely neutralized Garfiel.

"..."

Touching the wound on his forehead, breathing hard, Garfiel performed the ritual of calming himself. Slow, deep breaths steadied his heart and lungs, and he reined in thoughts that threatened to be swept away by rage. What he needed to think about wasn't the skill with which Otto kept perfectly trapping him.

It was why Otto was throwing himself into such a reckless fight in the first place.

To begin with, it made no sense for Otto to be challenging Garfiel at all. His objective was to buy time — to draw Garfiel's attention to himself while, in the meantime, the evacuees remaining in the Sanctuary escaped one after another by separate routes. That was exactly what Otto himself had explained, spelling it out. And if his words were true, then even if Garfiel gave chase now, there was no way he could stop every single dragon carriage.

For a moment, Garfiel considered using his authority to send Ryuzu's copies after them, but unless he knew the exact departure point of each carriage, it would amount to nothing but wasted effort. The copies had no knowledge or experience to draw on and could only carry out the crudest of instructions. Worse, he had to remind them even to eat regularly, and once they hit their activity limit in the forest, they'd curl up into little balls and try to give up on living. Hunting them down before they disappeared for good was something Garfiel was already sick to death of.

"In the end, the only one I can rely on is myself. Ha! Ain't that always the way."

His options were limited, and his nose was blocked. Even so, Garfiel wasn't discouraged. He had this body he'd honed to perfection. He had more than enough strength left to tear through the forest and accomplish his goal.

Whatever Otto's objective was, the man had resisted him this far — surely he'd already accepted that he might draw Garfiel's fury and feel tooth and claw for it. Garfiel had stopped thinking of Otto as mere prey to simply be devoured. He judged him instead as a quarry that needed to be hunted with everything he had, and resolved to run him down and finish him without fail.

—The very fact that he was thinking this way meant Garfiel had already forgotten his original objective, and he didn't even notice how thoroughly he was being led along by Otto's scheme.

"Real well-behaved of you two, ain'tcha. Don't tell me — he told you to stay put too, that it?"

Turning to track down Otto hiding in the forest, Garfiel glanced over at the standing dragon carriages — two decoys set up to look like they were carrying evacuees to safety. That said, the two earth dragons pulling them were real, and ever since the exchange between Otto and Garfiel had begun, they'd knelt down right where they stood, maintaining a posture of complete indifference.

"Smart of you not to move — means I got no reason to hurt you. Not that I go around killin' things that don't need killin', anyway."

Shaking his head, Garfiel slipped past the earth dragons and laid his hands on the carriage's cabin once more. Inside, a great pile of clothing had been scattered about to confuse the villagers' scent trail. Earlier he'd only checked that much before moving on, but if he dug a bit longer, he might find something.

Kicking aside the scattered clothes with his foot, Garfiel scanned the seats and the edges of the walls. Finding nothing of note, he was about to give up his brief search and step out of the cabin when—

"—Ahn?"

Turning back, his eyes caught something stuck as if hidden behind the cabin door. Fluttering in the wind was a white piece of paper, positioned so it could only be seen from the inside.

—A bad feeling crept over him. Garfiel strode up to the door, roughly tore the fluttering paper free, and spread it open in his hand.

And then—

*'You put on such a show for me — makes it all worth doing.'*

Reading those written words, Garfiel's vision flooded red with fury.

The next instant — the seat inside the cabin flipped up, and a black mass exploded within the cramped little room. Garfiel's furious roar was swallowed by the storm-like drone of wings as a swarm of insects burst forth.

—To young Otto Suwen, the world had been a cradle of hell.

"..."
"×××××××××"

"＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊＊！＊！＊"

Around the clock, without a moment's rest, Otto's ears kept picking up words whose meaning he could never grasp. Sitting slack-jawed on the ground, the world forced its connection on him constantly — whispered close to his ear, shouted from far away, sometimes sung, sometimes howled like a death cry.

No matter where in the world he went, the voices chased little Otto down without fail. Days with no respite. An endless, discordant chorus echoing on and on. A concerto of hell in which nothing, not a single part, had any intention of harmonizing with anything else, clung constantly at Otto's side.

—Why did everyone else live as though this unbearably noisy world were perfectly ordinary?

In the midst of a hell where he couldn't even make out the voice of the person beside him, Otto harbored that question. His parents would lift him up and say something to him with smiling faces. But no matter how much love those words carried, they were swallowed up by a sea of noise before they ever reached Otto's ears.

Noticing their son's abnormality, his parents quickly took him to see doctors. He never laughed, never got angry, never cried. What passed for emotional expression simply never developed in him, because every stimulus from the outside world felt identical to Otto — indistinguishable from all the rest. And so, to his parents' growing concern, Otto spent his early childhood as what looked like an utterly emotionless child.

Fortunately, perhaps, the Suwen family ran a merchant house that maintained a comfortably middle-class standard of living, and they had more than enough savings to keep taking their son to doctors. But no matter which doctor examined Otto's body, nothing abnormal was ever found. Of course not — if he had to put a name to his condition, it was nothing other than deafness caused by hearing too much.

He had an older brother two years his senior and a younger brother two years his junior. Unlike Otto, his brothers grew up perfectly healthy, thriving under their parents' love. Their parents' attention gradually drifted away from Otto; the affection meant for three children was divided between two, and Otto was pushed further and further from his parents' warmth.

Otto held no resentment or envy toward his brothers or parents for this. Partly it was because, at that time, he hadn't yet developed the capacity to hate or envy anyone — neither positive nor negative emotions had taken root in him yet — and partly because his brothers patiently kept him company even though words barely reached him. As for his parents growing weary at heart, he could accept that too, as simply unavoidable. If he'd been in either of his brothers' shoes back then, he wasn't sure he could have been so devotedly kind to a family member who made no sense to him. If anything, he was grateful for it.

Even if voices couldn't reach him as sound, he could communicate through writing. It was his older brother — who had tried to read books aloud to Otto — who discovered that.

Learning to read and write was, naturally, an immense struggle. After all, the sounds needed to recognize words simply didn't get through to him. Understanding what a string of words meant took Otto ten times as long as it took an ordinary child learning to read. And yet it wasn't painful for him. Sadly, he simply hadn't developed the sensitivity to find it painful, and an infant who couldn't live a normal life had nothing but time on his hands anyway.

*'—Thank you, always.'*

Otto still remembers vividly, even now, the moment he first showed his parents those words written on paper, and how they burst into tears and held him close.

He hadn't clearly understood the feeling of gratitude yet, but he knew he was receiving treatment that called for it. Judging things that way, young as he was, he wrote the words out of a sense of obligation — and when they were met with such a reaction, something stirred for the first time in Otto's heart.

—The wailing that followed, loud enough to scrape his throat raw, was perhaps the first time he'd cried out like that since the moment of his birth. If so, it was Otto's second birth-cry.

"Fwagatoku gaotai jijiji"
"AGATEGATAGFATTTETAADAERTERA"
"Mee-mee-moo-mee-may-mee-mee"

It was shortly after that second birth-cry that Otto began, dimly, to find patterns in the hellish chorus whose meaning he could never grasp. The countless noises that had ceaselessly battered his eardrums began, little by little but with growing certainty, to be sorted and filtered out by his own will.

And by the time Otto turned eight years old, he had become fully able to block out the surrounding sounds through sheer will alone. Having become, for all practical purposes, an ordinary child, he began from that point to soak up everything around him greedily, like water poured onto a parched desert.

Otto had, after all, been forced to forgo most of what a child ordinarily learns by the age of eight. He'd mastered letters and applied himself diligently to his studies, but his comprehension still lagged somewhat behind children his own age. He closed that gap in one stroke, using concentration as his weapon.

The talent that had lain dormant in the boy named Otto Suwen bloomed. His powers of thought and comprehension came to rival, even surpass, those of his brothers. Displaying a rare gift for learning, Otto rapidly rose to prominence among the boys and girls of his generation—

—and then proceeded to fail spectacularly at human relationships and isolate himself completely.

"Why does everyone live like it's normal, in such a difficult world?"

Slapped across the face by a girl he'd taken a liking to, his cheek red and swollen, Otto hugged his knees and muttered to himself. Having turned ten, Otto had been throwing himself into his studies so as not to bring shame on his family as a merchant's son. In this age, an environment where a child could receive a solid education from an early age wasn't common; he was, without question, fortunate, living an ideal life among boys and girls his own age. The problem was that Otto's emotional expression and mental maturity lagged about seven years behind children his age.

Having never made the ordinary mistakes most children make as a matter of course, Otto — once he finally became capable of making them — went on to make them as a matter of course too. But most such mistakes are only forgiven when made in the earliest years of childhood; when Otto, well past that window, committed the same blunders, the results could only be described as disasters.

To make matters worse, Otto Suwen was a boy thoroughly blessed with misfortune. According to his parents, Otto's bad luck got its start right after birth, when he nearly drowned in his birth-bath. From there, without any intention on his part, misfortune assailed him relentlessly — pushed down stairs, hit by bird droppings, nearly drowned in a flower vase. He'd had little awareness of it at the time, simply because the capacity to feel misfortune as misfortune hadn't developed in him yet.

Once that capacity did develop and he looked back on his past, Otto was appalled at his own track record. Just what did a person have to do to be so thoroughly abandoned by luck?

"Big, passed, now, passed, already, gone."
"Shining, shone, the light, far away, light, light, shining."
"Hey, a demon's comin'. Hey, a demon's comin'."

Around this same time, a change came over the noise he'd learned to consciously block out. The meaningless chorus that had never carried any meaning began to feel as though it did carry meaning after all. Even once he could make most of it out, much of it remained incomprehensible — but through relentless effort turning incomprehension into understanding, Otto discovered the true nature of the hell of his childhood.

It seemed he had the power to communicate with living creatures other than humankind. It took Otto eleven years from the manifestation of this ability — later identified as the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language — to finally become aware of it.

From then on, to learn the limits of the Divine Protection granted to him, Otto visited all manner of places around town to test it. Through trial and error, he found that the more intelligent the creature, the more clearly its will came through to him. Eventually, he demonstrated to his brothers that he could converse with the earth dragon kept at home, and confessed that he'd possessed this Divine Protection since early childhood.

"Y-yeah, I see. Yeah... um, listen... Otto. That power is, yeah, amazing. Yeah, I really think it's amazing, so... um, what I mean is. You mustn't use it where people can see."

Possessing a Divine Protection was a blessing from the world, but that didn't mean the bearer of such a skill was welcomed by everyone. If it were a Divine Protection that benefited everyone, that would be one thing, but Otto's power only worked for Otto himself, and even a child's mind could think up all sorts of ways it might be abused. His brother's concern for him, born of worry, was something Otto could genuinely understand.

Promising his brother, whose face had gone pale as he looked away, Otto firmly resolved that no one around him must ever learn of his Divine Protection of the Soul of Language. This power might endanger not only himself but the people around him too. In young Otto's heart, a sense of duty had kindled — he had to protect his precious family.

It was only three days after that promise to his brother that the existence of the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language became known, and Otto was thoroughly ostracized by the boys and girls his age.

Caught secretly talking with the earth dragon by his younger brother, Otto had no choice but to confess his Divine Protection to him as well. He told him, too, about his older brother's concern, and decided to share with his younger brother just how dangerous this power truly was. But the next day, his younger brother — eager to show off Otto's power — brought a large crowd of children together, and Otto was seen conversing with insects. For the first time in years, he saw hell again.

The drawback of the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language was that, to communicate, he had to use the other party's own language. Put simply, when Otto conversed with an earth dragon, he whinnied like one; when he conversed with insects, he made insect-like cries.

The nickname "the clueless Zodda Bug guy" spread in an instant. From then on, Otto resolved to seal away the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language and never use it again. It took him several years, but he succeeded in erasing the bad reputation that had stuck to him and wiping the unfortunate memory from most people's minds. He accomplished this at fourteen — an impressionable age.

By fourteen, excuses about a still-developing heart no longer held water. His body, too, was steadily climbing the stairway to adulthood, and by the time his limbs had finished stretching out, young Otto had grown into a face and figure that could fairly be called handsome. Gray hair, a soft face with a touch of the world-weary about it, gentle eyes, and a temperament devoted earnestly to whatever he did. Surprisingly well-equipped with qualities that tugged at maternal instincts, young Otto — like any boy his age — began to take an interest in matters of the heart—

—and, through the power of the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language, made an enemy of the daughter of the most powerful man in town, resulting in his banishment. This happened just before young Otto turned fifteen, in the cold season.

The details can be skipped over, but in short, he got caught up in a drama of love and jealousy. On the night of a birthday party held for the daughter of the town's most powerful man, her boyfriend stormed in, furious, shouting that she'd been seen with some other man. The one singled out was Otto, who'd been seen speaking with the daughter shortly before. Otto answered honestly that she'd only asked him the time, but the man, his face red with rage, hurling "You Zodda Bug bastard!" at him, wasn't in any mood to listen.

Having his past — which he thought he'd shaken off four years ago — dredged back up, even Otto lost his composure this once. And so he broke his own seal, and in a desperate bid to clear the suspicion cast on him, listened in on every living creature in town, uncovering the fact that on the night in question, the girl had actually been carrying on with no fewer than seven other men. He then cheerfully informed the pitiable man: "It seems you're number eight!"

Beaten by the man and then targeted by an assassin hired by the girl whose infidelity had just been exposed, Otto fled his hometown in a mad scramble, relying on a connection of his father's to get work at an acquaintance's trading firm. From there he trained as an apprentice, and set out as a traveling merchant at sixteen — Otto Suwen's independence as a man had begun.

Otto's journey as a traveling merchant was, without exaggeration, one hardship after another. His knack for misfortune didn't loosen its grip on him even as the years passed. Carry fragile goods, and he'd be hit by foul weather; try to cut a trip short through the mountains, and he'd be attacked by bandits; camp for the night with fellow merchants, and Otto alone would have every drop of blood in his body drained by insects. Sadly, the only reason he survived all this misfortune without being utterly ruined was that he'd been blessed with just enough business acumen to counterbalance it.

He never made big profits, but he never suffered crushing losses either. Steering by an almost miraculously balanced sense of plus-minus-zero — depressingly modest for a merchant — his four years until turning twenty passed in a flash. What kept him from breaking down and crawling home was Frufoo, the earth dragon he'd known since childhood, whom he'd brought with him from home on the day he was exiled.

Honestly, Otto had a lot of complicated feelings about Frufoo, given that the dragon had been the trigger that exposed his Divine Protection to his brothers, but by now Frufoo was a genuine bond for him — something like precious family. For some reason, Otto found it hard to get other merchants to travel with him, and he often whiled away his sleepless solitary nights talking with Frufoo.

He'd cling to the conversation even as Frufoo, embarrassed, tried to beg off with "let me sleep already." That other merchants, keeping their distance, would give him a wide berth as he sat by the fire at night imitating an earth dragon's cries and talking to it, was, by this point, simply to be expected.

Into these unremarkable days — a life that looked, from the outside, like a child's harmless little gamble, but was, in truth, quite a desperate struggle for the man living it — a turning point arrived for Otto.

—He missed a business opportunity, and utterly blundered it.

At the time, Otto was dealing in oil. He'd been told by a ruddy-faced, eyepatch-wearing, bearded man that as the cold season approached, oil fetched an extraordinarily high price in the northern country of Gusteko. Otto traded away the ironware he was carrying for oil and set off for Gusteko in high spirits — only to run headlong into a sudden severance of diplomatic relations, leaving him with no way to unload his goods. What struck an even harder blow to his heart was hearing, afterward, that the ironware he'd sold off at a steep discount was fetching an enormous price back in the royal capital.

Otto realized he'd been thoroughly played for a fool, and that his career as a traveling merchant was in mortal danger. If he couldn't somehow find a chance to turn things around and get back on his feet, he might end up having to give up Frufoo. Worse still, he might end up crawling back to his family in tears.

For Otto, that was a line he could never allow himself to cross. More than five years had passed since he'd last seen his family, yet his heart still held not the slightest diminishment of affection for them. That he was even alive today, in whatever fashion, was thanks to the family who hadn't given up on him during his difficult childhood. In those ten hellish early years, Otto had already caused his family a lifetime's worth of trouble. From here on, he had to spend the rest of his life paying back that debt.

Debts must be settled precisely and correctly. After all, Otto Suwen was a merchant's son.

—A fellow merchant he knew approached him with talk of a lucrative deal, and Otto took the bait.

The job, apparently, didn't call for merchandise but for an earth dragon's legs. They wanted to load people into the bed of a dragon carriage and move a large number of them. Otto jumped at it without a second thought, pushing Frufoo and his Divine Protection of the Soul of Language to their absolute limits, determined to arrive at the meeting point faster than anyone else.

Tearing across rough terrain, cutting through pathless wilderness, pushing past even Frufoo's protesting "Let's just forget this, young master," Otto arrived faster than anyone else.

And then—

"Well, well, well... in such a hurry, where might you be off to... I wonder!"

Everything went horribly wrong.

Captured by a group whose eyes had clearly gone somewhere else entirely, trussed up like a rolled mat, Otto realized his misfortune had, at long last, reached its true peak. Torn away from Frufoo and stripped of his belongings, he was thrown into a freezing cave, left as nothing more than something waiting for its life to be taken on a whim.

Who could possibly understand the depth of despair that soaked through Otto's heart at that moment? No one, surely. Because even then, Otto had exerted every ounce of power he had to try to escape, unleashing the Divine Protection of the Soul of Language at full force, searching for any way out of that group's clutches. What broke his rebellious spirit was overwhelming silence — when he fully unleashed the Divine Protection, a hell equal to the one that had assailed him as a child should have unfolded before him.

That nostalgic, hateful chorus of noise — he heard none of it at all. Insects, small animals, all the creatures that should exist practically everywhere, in a forest or a cave — every one of them had gone into hiding, as though from some demonic presence. Otto, who had steeled himself for hell, witnessed a hell that surpassed even his worst preparations, and his heart shattered completely.

The light drained from his eyes, and strength rapidly left his entire body. He understood: it was all over now. Nothing he did would work out, and in the end he would simply meet his end in this freezing cave. In a despair so total it left no room even for tears, as he lost track of time in the hollow reality before him, it arrived without warning — the thing that scooped up Otto Suwen's fate.

"What's this! You Witch Cult idiots doin' whatever the hell you please without a care! What a buncha useless bastards!"

A loud voice echoed through the cave, and Otto, lost in a daze, was yanked back to reality. He lifted his face and called for help in a hoarse voice. What appeared, drawn by his cry, was a hulking beastman with a dog's face, speaking in a Kararagi accent, who freed the captive Otto.

"You're one lucky fella! If we hadn't shown up, you'd have been done for, no question! Any later and it'd have been the same story! Just in the nick of time! Nick of time! And hey, you oughta thank the boss's kid too, y'know!"

"T-the boss's... kid...?"

Rotating his bound wrists and ankles, Otto tilted his head at the beastman's boisterous laughter. The beastman blinked in surprise at Otto's confusion, then slapped him hard on the back with a massive palm, making Otto yelp, and said,

"Boss is boss! Kid is kid! Put 'em together, boss's kid! He's the one who gave us the orders, sent us all the way out here! Didn't look like he had that kinda head on his shoulders, but you really can't judge a book by its cover! Gahahaha!"

"I-I see... th-thank you. I understand. Anyway, thank you so much. Oh, right, I should thank him too—"

Looking up to offer his thanks, Otto suddenly noticed something. The beastman was looking at his face with a startled, grimacing expression. Not understanding the reason for the reaction, Otto watched as the beastman, surprisingly, pulled a white handkerchief from inside his clothes and tossed it to him.

"What, if you're gonna cry, go hide somewhere and do it. Ain't dignified for a man to sniffle in front of folks."

"Eh, huh... c-cry?"

"Tears are just pourin' outta you! What d'you call that if not cryin'! Sweat!? Sweat from your heart!? Even in Kararagi nobody makes that kinda joke anymore, pal!"

The beastman turned his back on Otto and stepped away, almost as if out of consideration. Half in disbelief, Otto pressed the cloth to his face, and was struck with genuine astonishment to find it soaking up a torrent of tears. And once he'd become aware of the tears, they came pouring out one after another, without end.

"Ah, damn it... w-why am I... like this... like this...!"

Unable to stem the flood, Otto gritted his teeth and pressed the cloth to his face. Not understanding the source of his own tears, his mind was overrun with incoherent curses.

—Was it because he'd been freed from a despair so deep even tears had dried up, that the tears now came?

"I— I'm glad... I didn't die... I'm so glad...!"

He hadn't accomplished anything yet. He hadn't repaid even a fraction of the debt he owed.

If he had died there, Otto would have ended without ever fulfilling the meaning of his own birth. Having survived, here and now, Otto was able to grasp that truth.

—Every time Otto's life brought him to tears, he felt himself being reborn.

The first birth-cry, when he'd first come into this world.

The second birth-cry, when he'd learned his family's love and found the place where his heart belonged.

And now the third — passing through a death he'd resigned himself to, and coming to understand the purpose and meaning of living.

—On this day, Otto Suwen let out another birth-cry.

"—Truth be told, nobody actually asked me to buy this much time."

Kicking off the ground, throwing himself into manual labor that didn't suit him one bit, Otto let a wry smile cross his face. The memory of himself bawling like an unsightly mess was embarrassing enough that he'd rather forget it — but unfortunately, every memory of him crying was precious to him, and try as he might, he couldn't forget any of them.

That time, the beastman named Ricardo who had saved Otto had kept it a secret from everyone that Otto had bawled his eyes out. That debt was one he would have to repay someday, without fail.

And so—

"A debt owed must always be repaid. After all — I'm a merchant."


—The boss's kid, who saved his life.

Otto Suwen had a debt he had to repay to Natsuki Subaru too. To answer the debt of having his life saved, by pouring in everything he had — that was simply the natural mindset of a merchant.

And above all—

"—It's for a friend, that's why!!"

Both Otto the merchant and Otto the man had set themselves the task of holding the line, here and now. And so Otto Suwen threw himself into a fight with slim odds of success. Taking a gamble that disregarded the odds entirely, he would stack everything his own existence could add onto the pile, toward Natsuki Subaru's victory.

That was Otto's merchant's spirit, and proof of his friendship.


—Far off, from the direction of the dragon carriages he'd left behind, he heard a beast's furious roar rise up.


Sensing that real battle was truly about to be joined, Otto unleashed his own Divine Protection — surrendering himself once more to that familiar hell — and kept running, to wring out every last ounce of strength he had.
