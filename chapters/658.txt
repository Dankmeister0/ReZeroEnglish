Arc 8 Chapter 42: "The Great Slave"

—Priscilla Barielle does not scorn the Stargazers.

Pitiable beings afflicted with a sickness of the mind, appearing nowhere but in the Vollachian Empire.

That was the common perception among those who had any familiarity with the Stargazers—not that one frequently encountered those who had developed this so-called dialogue with the world to begin with.

Even so, there were positions in life that made one more likely to cross paths with someone afflicted by Stargazing.

Those close to the Vollachian imperial family, for instance.

Whether it was the imperials themselves, or those who attended to their daily needs—stewards, bodyguards, and others in such positions—there was a chance they might come across someone afflicted with Stargazing.

After all, the Stargazers seemed to be desperate, one and all, to insert themselves into the Empire's history—whenever some great event worthy of being recorded in the imperial annals loomed, they would come flocking to tattle about it.

That said, there was no precedent of the words of a Stargazer—scorned as they were as a breed of madmen—ever being taken seriously.

No doubt there had been the occasional eccentric who lent an ear to a Stargazer's words, but at the very least, not a single history book on record had ever noted, publicly, that such tattling had been of any use.

That had been the common understanding of the relationship between the Empire and its Stargazers—right up until the current emperor, Vincent Vollachia, granted Ubilk a post and the freedom to come and go from the castle.

Was it not as though the Stargazers were suitors, pining after the Empire with an ardor forever unreturned?

Suitors granted a heavenly mandate, who blindly cast everything aside in pursuit of its fulfillment. Most of the Empire's people who knew of them held them in contempt—but Priscilla did not scorn them. Nor did she pity them.

The Stargazers were nothing so special as to merit either scorn or pity.

To begin with, most living things were slaves to something greater than themselves.

Whether that something was a king, a family, a spouse, or love, hatred, fate—only the object of one's servitude differed.

And that included—

"—even I am no exception."

With a jangle of the chains binding her manacles, she murmured those words into the darkness.

When she spun words, she rarely cared whether anyone was listening—but at least this murmur had not been spoken to herself; it had been meant for someone else's ears.

Though it seemed the other party had no wish to oblige her, for there came no reply.

Still, if they thought to hide their presence, that petty wish would go unfulfilled.

"No matter how you hold your breath, a presence such as yours cannot be concealed. Or must I go to the trouble of walking over myself before you'll deign to show your face?"

"...As long as those manacles remain, that would be an impossible act."

"I imagined as much. Then you have no choice but to come to me. I have no intention of exchanging words with someone who won't show their face."

"——"

There was a beat—not quite hesitation, but a moment of thought before the footsteps came. Then, slowly, the sound of shoes striking the cold floor approached, and a small figure emerged from the darkness.

Light was scarce in the dungeon, and Priscilla could make out little more than a hazy impression of the newcomer's shape.

There was a limit to how well one's eyes could adjust to the dark—and besides, she hadn't bothered closing her eyes beforehand just to prepare for the gloom.

"I do not stoop to skulking about in lightless dark. When I make my exit, I do so in the light, and boldly."

"How very like you—an arrogant way of putting it. Requires: caution."

No sooner had that emotionless voice sounded than something harder than a heel struck sharply against the stone floor. A high sound sliced through the cold air, and then a pale blue light forced back the darkness of the underground chamber.

"——"

In that instant, rising up within the light was something white and beautiful—

Before Priscilla, chained in the depths below, stood a girl with long pink hair holding a pale blue glowing staff—true to the telltale cracked skin and ghastly eyes, an Undead.

If there was a difference, it was that this Undead—wearing the form of a small child, the first Priscilla had ever laid eyes on—was none other than—

"—the mastermind behind this Great Disaster."

"I won't deny it, but where did you hear that term? I didn't think you'd have had the chance."

"It was your man who brought me my meals, was it not? Did you starve him for conversation? He rattled on about all manner of things to pass the time, whether I asked to hear it or not."

"Requires: warning, for First General Temeglyph. But—"

There, the young Undead cut her words short and closed the distance to Priscilla by a single step.

Even so, she remained far off. However elegantly long Priscilla's legs might be, even lifting one wouldn't let her reach over and fix the girl's bangs.

"Did you manage? To endure the tedium."

"——"

Keeping that distance from Priscilla, the young Undead continued past the words she'd cut off.

At the question, Priscilla narrowed her eyes slightly. There was a strange closeness to the child's words, as if she were peering directly into Priscilla's face.

Not a physical closeness, but a closeness of the spirit.

"You speak as though you've known me for some time."

"I wonder. Do you remember me? Do you have any recollection of me at all?"

"Unfortunately, I am not so eccentric as to remember something so trifling. Either you are not worth remembering, or this is the first time you've shown me that face of yours."

"Correct. This is the first time you have seen my face."

The nodding child's answer, however, was not a complete resolution of Priscilla's suspicion.

But precisely because the remark left something snagging in her mind yet again, the other's true intent was clear—she was testing Priscilla, watching to see whether the tethered beast would bite at the bait.

Sensing that intent, Priscilla gave a small snort.

"One might call this fearless insolence, to show toward me."

"Indeed, I know nothing of the emotion called fear. Nor is fear the only thing I don't know—though as a substitute for my mother, a servant to the thirst for knowledge, this is a grave—"

"So you know me one-sidedly. —You're the specter behind that village, Coffleton."

"...Requires: explanation."

Cut off mid-flow in her flat self-narration, the child caught her breath at Priscilla naming what she recognized.

That trailing murmur was proof that what Priscilla recognized was indeed connected to the child—but she seemed unable to grasp how Priscilla had made the connection.

"Hmph, it's hardly a difficult matter, is it? Once before, in my domain back in the Kingdom, an incident occurred similar to what's happening now in the capital—no, across the whole Empire. When such an oddity crosses one's path twice, the two naturally link together."

That incident, which had occurred in the Barielle domain of the Kingdom of Lugunica, had centered on a village called Coffleton, and its substance had been the village's residents turning into Undead.

Though at the time, it could hardly be called reviving the dead—it would be more accurate to say the corpses were simply being moved about at someone's will. The queen who had been at the root of it was defeated, and with no further reports of similar phenomena since, the matter had been considered settled.

"It seems you simply moved your nest and continued your 'test.' That ghastly game of puppetry, slipping insects into corpses—it appears you've improved on it a little."

"My previous approach had too many obstacles to achieving my goal. Requires: improvement."

"So you won't deny your involvement with Coffleton."

"I see no point in it. Wouldn't that be a meaningless exchange?"

The child's response, betraying no emotion, was less a dislike of inefficient acts than an outright aversion to them.

Setting aside her lack of contrition, the absence of denial was, at least, useful for extracting the information Priscilla wanted. But at the same time, an exchange with such a partner—

"—is utterly, thoroughly tedious, you."

"Is that important?"

"Setting aside whether there's any benefit to it, that is the one point where a conversation might find value."

"Then perhaps you needn't set aside the question of benefit at all?"

"That is exactly why I say conversing with you is tedious. It's like talking to the dead. I daresay speaking to a gravestone would be preferable—at least it wouldn't bother me with troublesome replies."

"——"

Narrowing her black eyes at Priscilla's words, the child closed the distance by another step. Then, pressing the hand not holding the staff to her chest, she said,

"Not 'like'—this truly is a dialogue with the dead. Surely, seeing my form, even you wouldn't take this for a conversation with the living."

"It would be crass to force something that can't properly be called 'alive' into the categories of life and death. Though I admit, it was somewhat surprising that you turned out to be an Undead."

"...Why is that?"

"For the mastermind behind this rite to become an Undead herself—that can only be called a reckless gamble. There's even the risk that the moment you died, the spell you'd spun would break, and all your scheming would come to nothing."

Of course, there was a chance such worry was groundless, owing to some information or reasoning Priscilla wasn't privy to. But considering this child's nature, there remained ample room for doubt.

This girl was, by nature, averse to inefficiency, and not one to reach for anything she judged to have low certainty.

And yet, she had treated even her own life as merely one card among many in bringing about the Great Disaster.

"Did you face an unavoidable 'death,' and so had no choice but to entrust your hopes to the afterlife? Or were you already an Undead before setting this incident in motion? If neither of those—"

"If neither?"

"—Then perhaps you accepted that, should nothing follow after the end of your life, ending there would be acceptable enough."

Guessing at the incomprehensible true intent of the child turned Undead, Priscilla voiced three possibilities.

In order, saving for last the possibility that, based on the child's nature as Priscilla had analyzed it thus far, seemed the least likely to be her true intent.

And yet—

"Requires: praise."

The child gave that response to Priscilla's guess precisely when she voiced what should have been the least likely possibility of all.

Which was to say, nothing less than that the child's scheme, which had its roots around the time of the Coffleton incident, could be allowed to collapse the very instant her own life ended—and she had been prepared to accept that.

That fact alone was enough to make Priscilla's beautiful brow furrow, but what made it doubly so was the child's reaction to having her true intent guessed correctly.

With her pale, lifeless face, the child smiled as though her wish had been granted.

Witnessing that smile in the light cast by the staff, Priscilla, brow furrowed in earnest, said,

"So you've finally shown value enough to talk to, setting aside the question of benefit."

"...Has something changed?"

"If you cannot notice it yourself, then I shall offer my congratulations. Though for life to sprout after death is a touch too ironic for my taste..."

There, Priscilla paused and closed one of her crimson eyes. A beat passed—a moment of beautiful, noble contemplation—and then she opened the closed lid once more.

And in those crimson eyes, she captured clearly the face of the child, whose smile had vanished,

"No, that's not it. It didn't sprout after death. You cast aside your own life in order to reveal it, didn't you?"

"I did not take my own life. The opportunity simply came around on its own. So your guess that I faced an unavoidable 'death' isn't wrong either. Requires: correction."

Even as she answered, the child's lips curved once more into that same smile as before.

In that reaction, Priscilla clearly sensed the child's pulse of life—the presence of emotion. It was, in all likelihood, something the child hadn't possessed before.

And without that, the dead could not be revived as the Undead.

And so the child had proven it with her own life.

That she possessed the qualification to become an Undead—a reason to reinsert her soul into a vessel of earth and cling to this present life.

It had been a plan of low certainty, one that could hardly be called efficient by any measure.

"But is it not emotion that drives one to make such a choice?"

"Just so."

With her hand still pressed to her chest, the child now carried about her something almost like defiance.

The former sense of hollowness—as if conversing with an empty doll—had vanished, replaced now by the tension of facing something decisively different in nature from something inanimate.

"My former self had my plan in the Kingdom thwarted precisely because I made light of this. And though I failed to become a 'Witch' of the Kingdom... it seems I have been chosen as the Empire's 'Great Disaster' instead."

"'Chosen,' is it? Do you mean to say that's the reason you're inviting calamity?"

"Requires: correction. That is not a motive. It is a basis."

An answer, unhesitating, to Priscilla's question.

Listening to the child's reply, Priscilla began giving shape to a suspicion that had until then been vague and formless.

That suspicion, sunk in a shadow darker even than the dungeon's own gloom, was gradually growing clear through the child's words and expression, just as she had brought the staff's light down into the depths.

And then—

"—My name is Sphinx. In the Kingdom of Lugunica, I was also called a 'Witch.'"

"——"

So it was that the child—Sphinx—stared straight at Priscilla and gave her name, and only now did the meaning behind her words and manner finally reach Priscilla.

That she had bothered to add the qualifier "in the Kingdom of Lugunica" was surely to draw a clear distinction from others called 'Witch' in other countries.

The six Witches who had once existed besides the Witch of Envy had all but faded from history, yet they remained figures known the world over, in common, as 'Witches.'

But if one deliberately narrowed it down to the 'Witch' of the Kingdom of Lugunica, only one fit the bill.

That she was the one who had taken part in the Demi-Human War was easy enough to learn by unraveling history.

But what Sphinx, who now called herself this 'Witch,' wanted to convey to Priscilla was not some trivial piece of information like her own identity.

What the 'Witch' wanted Priscilla to understand was—

"—that the reason you overcame a meaningless death and gained the sprout of life that let you become an Undead... is me."

It was a declaration of war: that the reason Sphinx had become the Great Disaster lay with Priscilla.

To begin with, the situation had been too blatant to need thinking over.

In the decisive battle at the capital, where the Imperial Army and the rebel forces had clashed head-on, Sphinx had led her Undead into the fray as the Great Disaster, muddying the outcome of the civil war.

In the midst of that battle, Priscilla and Yorna, both consumed by their clash with Arakiya, had fallen behind the other battlefields in grasping how events were unfolding, and as a result, had been left a step behind the Undead's scheme.

Now Priscilla found herself chained in a dungeon, with no way of knowing whether Arakiya or Yorna were even safe.

The effects of the Soul Marriage Technique that Yorna had woven into Priscilla during the battle against Arakiya still lingered, so there was little doubt Yorna remained alive. Even setting that aside, Priscilla had resigned herself to captivity in exchange for the safety of the unconscious Arakiya and the shaken Yorna, thrown into disarray by the Undead's sudden appearance.

That bargain could not have been struck unless the other side had intended to capture Priscilla in the first place.

Which was to say, the very fact that Priscilla now sat alive and chained in a cell meant, as a matter of course, that the other side had business with her.

"I had assumed it was Lamia who was so set on sparing my life."

"Princess Lamia Godwin was indeed in favor of sparing your life as well. She was strongly fixated on you. In her final moments—"

"—I have no intention of sharing what passed between Lamia and me with anyone."

Priscilla dismissed it outright as vulgar curiosity.

At Priscilla's flat assertion, Sphinx withdrew easily with a simple, "Is that so." She had broached the topic, but her interest evidently didn't lie there.

That much was clear from the fact that Priscilla had been kept alive even after Lamia's second death.

"Attachment is a curious thing, isn't it? It's swayed far too much by factors that run counter to rationality. And yet, I found it difficult to understand how the irrational could sometimes outweigh the rational."

"And is speaking with me like this the result of weighing rationality, or irrationality?"

"I wonder which it is. Requires: consideration... though the very act of doing so feels novel in itself."

Was Sphinx, answering so, even aware of it herself?

Aware or not, Sphinx, even as she spoke with Priscilla here and now, was rapidly gaining humanity in her words.

Water, in the form of this dialogue with Priscilla, was being poured onto the sprout of her budding humanity, and it was growing by leaps and bounds.

"——"

The true intent Priscilla had discerned in Sphinx, once voiced, went undenied.

Sphinx's evident reluctance to press further into the matter amounted, in itself, to a clear confirmation that it lay at the core of her motive.

The one point on which Priscilla found herself, frustratingly, a step behind Sphinx was this: she had not the faintest recollection of anything that could account for Sphinx's fierce attachment to her.

Of course, someone in Priscilla's position was no stranger to being the object of one-sided attachment from people she'd never met, who knew nothing more of her than her name.

But Sphinx's attachment clearly went well beyond mere self-satisfied fixation.

There was a reason. The very reason that had set the Great Disaster in motion.

"You said something interesting a moment ago."

"——"

"That all living things are slaves to something greater than themselves. I couldn't grasp that before, but now I sense the beginnings of an understanding."

Sphinx, who had begun with "interesting," was met with silence from Priscilla at her words.

It was neither dismissal nor contempt—if anything, it was a dangerous curiosity. Sphinx's remark, opening with "interesting," was itself something Priscilla found interesting in turn.

What sort of tale would this 'Witch,' who now showed understanding of what she once hadn't, and had even voiced agreement with Priscilla's words, go on to tell?

Sure enough, prompted by Priscilla's silence, Sphinx continued.

"Precisely because of that, I can find a new rationality within the irrational. Requires: attention."

So saying, Sphinx once more struck the floor sharply with the tip of her glowing staff. The flicker of the orb set into it swelled larger than before, and in an instant, a change came over its surface.

—Within the pale, transparent orb, a view of the imperial capital outside the dungeon appeared.

Perhaps it worked on the same principle as a communication mirror, capturing the other side through its surface. Sensing a spell far too elaborate for mere long-distance viewing, Priscilla narrowed her eyes at the light.

Sphinx had told her to pay attention, but—

"What is it you wish to show me?"

"The correctness of your words, and the result of my new equation."

The correctness of Priscilla's words—if that was tied to what Sphinx had just said, Priscilla wondered what on earth would be shown, and then realized it.

That realization of Priscilla's came at the very same moment the orb's image sharpened into clarity.

The scene reflected there was—

"Emotion, and attachment—only once I understood them did I come to know how to make use of them. She's quite admirable, isn't she? For your sake, she spares nothing of herself. —Requires: consideration."

—At that very same moment, at the real location of the scene reflected in the orb.

"Ah, well, I've gone and done poor Groovy-san wrong, haven't I? I'd built up quite the reputation, in my own mind, for my instincts never missing at the crucial moment... and yet here I've missed completely."

The breezy manner of speech told of a blunder entirely at odds with the spirited tone of the words themselves.

And yet neither his voice nor his expression betrayed the slightest trace of discouragement. That was because he cared not one whit about his own blunder, and because his sense of apology wasn't especially sincere either—all owing to the conclusion that his intuition had been half right and half wrong.

Leaving the flashy work of drawing attention to an ally, he had raced across a capital overrun with Undead to the summit of the city walls—a point ranked and called "Number Two"—where the thing he sought should have been waiting.

Of course, it had been intuition alone that led him to that guess, and calling it certainty would no doubt earn him a scolding from plenty of people.

But at the very least, he himself had been certain. —That this was where the big scene would play out.

If he came here, then the brilliant exploits of Cecilus Segmunt, this world's leading man, could be put on display for the boisterous, clamoring audience watching over him.

And yet—

"...So, you got some kind of excuse?"

"Ah, well, let's see—how about this? My instincts weren't wrong at all. Because right here is exactly the thing I truly desire! How's that?"

Beside him, Al—holding the burnt remains of a leather hide that no longer served any purpose as a disguise—spoke resentfully, and Cecilus answered with high spirits.

In truth, whether that was even the case lay on a plane Cecilus couldn't judge—but wasn't believing in oneself so very much more positive than doubting oneself?

"Don't you agree, half-naked lady? A gloomy face invites a gloomy turn of events. Which means it goes without saying what expression a leading man basking in the light ought to wear."

"——"

Overhead, above the city wall he'd reached, floating still higher in the sky, a figure hung suspended—Cecilus raised his voice toward her, but there came no reply.

There had, however, been a first greeting from her—one that had turned the entire area into a sea of flame, meant to burn Cecilus and Al to ash where they hid beneath their leather hide.

And yet, even having done that much, not a trace of hostility or killing intent toward Cecilus or Al could be felt from her.

There was only, purely and simply, the sobbing plea of a girl whose slender body, its tan skin exposed, had taken in something vast enough to nearly burst it from within.

Just what circumstances had led that thing to enter her, he didn't know, but—

"I'd guess you swallowed something rather bad. —Really, you are a handful."

"——"

"Huh? What was that strange feeling just now..."

Before he could puzzle out what it was, there came movement overhead.

Light flickered, and a tremendous force came pouring down from above, bent on annihilating both Cecilus and Al. Facing it, Cecilus licked his lips, while beside him Al cast aside the leather hide and—

"Ah, dammit! —Domain Re-Expansion!!"

That desperate, half-abandoned cry was swallowed by the impact, and the greatest clash yet in the corpse-strewn capital began.
