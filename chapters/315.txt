One Day II Special 1: "Intersecting Wishes"

"Then, Your Highness, I'll take my leave. Please do take care of yourself."

Bowing her head reverently, the green-haired woman left the room, her hair swaying as she went.

She passed through the door held open by her attendant, and out in the corridor she turned to watch it swing slowly shut. Even knowing that a sound from the door wouldn't carry to the room beyond, she'd made a conscious effort to close it soundlessly — and now, at last, a sigh escaped her.

"See to it that His Highness wants for nothing," she said to the attendant standing beside the door, then turned away as if shaking off a wave of helpless emotion. She crossed the red-carpeted hallway, descended the stairs, and made her way to the hall.

"—Crusch-sama."

The voice, slightly breathless, calling out to her belonged to her companion, who had been waiting in the hall.

Ears like a cat's swaying with the sound of his voice — a young man whose features were so fine that he looked for all the world like a girl with a Demi-Human's traits greeted her with his yellow eyes going round.

His name was Felix Argyle. Known by the nickname Ferris, he was a trusted attendant and knight who had served the woman for many years.

"Thank you for your hard work. How was Prince Fourier looking?"

"Not very well, I'm afraid. A man who was once so full of vigor — there's no trace of that left in him now. Today especially, he kept lamenting that he wished he could go outside and walk beneath the sun. It's unbearably pitiful."

At the answer of the woman shaking her head — Crusch Karsten — Ferris's shoulders drooped.

Seeing his flaxen hair and the ears of the same color droop along with it, Crusch's lips eased into the faintest of smiles.

"There's no need for you to be so downhearted. This is a problem that every healer in the kingdom has been mobilized for, and still no progress has been made. You alone need not let your face cloud over it."

"But, but — that Feli-chan doesn't have enough strength to live up to Crusch-sama's expectations, that's a weakness of mine. And that's what's so frustrating... I'm sorry, Crusch-sama."

More than grieving over his inability to save the sick, he grieved over the grief he saw weighing on Crusch's heart for their sake.

Ferris's very way of being seemed to lean in close beside Crusch, and all she could do in answer to that devotion was close her eyes.

"Has the prince's — the royal family's — condition remained unchanged?"

"So has the diagnosis. Honestly, this is a conclusion I absolutely don't want to say out loud — not even as a healer of sorts, nya — but..."

Ferris's voice dropped, his eyes lowering. And yet, he didn't let himself hesitate over what came next. He said it plainly.

"—It's the Black Serpent's Venomous Tongue. There's no other way to explain this disease of unknown cause."

"If that's so, then where could they have contracted it from? ...No, never mind. There's no use debating that now. What happens from here?"

"In the worst case... the royal family will die out."

"—————"

It was a conclusion she had already reached many times before, and yet no matter how many times she heard it, she doubted she would ever grow used to it.

Crusch let out a breath through her nose, striving with all her might to settle her own heart.

The matter was, after all, an unprecedented one. If she lost her composure and let herself be swept up in emotion, there would be no undoing it. Even now, with the nobility thrown into deep confusion, the kingdom's functions were barely being held together — and that was only because the high nobility, Crusch included, and the Council of Wise Men were steering the ship.

—Even now, in this very moment, the royal bloodline of the Dragon Kingdom of Lugunica stood on the brink of extinction.

The first to be confirmed ill was the First Prince, Zabinel Lugunica. At first it had nearly been diagnosed as fatigue from his daily official duties, but within a few days Zabinel's condition took a sudden turn for the worse, and when other members of the royal family began to fall ill in the same way, the true scope of the problem came to light.

One after another, important figures tied to the royal bloodline collapsed under the illness, and for a time the royal castle teetered on the edge of utter, screaming chaos. What put a stop to it was the action taken by several members of the Council of Wise Men who happened to be staying at the castle, along with Crusch and Ferris, who were in attendance that day.

Ferris in particular was, in this matter, a practitioner of water magic said to have no equal in all of Lugunica — the pinnacle of healers, a man who bore the title of "Blue."

The royal family members who had suddenly collapsed were immediately carried into one wing of the castle, where emergency diagnosis and treatment were carried out.

But what was newly discovered there was the despairing truth that even Ferris's hands held no means of fighting back against this affliction.

In the span of mere days, the long and storied royal house of Lugunica now stood on the brink of ruin.

For now, the kingdom's most capable healers had been gathered, and for as long as time allowed, healing magic was being continuously applied to prolong the patients' lives — but this, too, was nothing more than a stopgap with no real solution behind it.

A gag order had been placed on the royal family's condition and the chaos within the castle, but how long that could be kept hidden was a matter of grave doubt.

"—————"

Turning over the grimness of the situation in her mind, Crusch thought of the person she had just gone to visit — the Fourth Prince of the Dragon Kingdom of Lugunica, Fourier Lugunica.

Fourier and Crusch were the same age, and as the daughter of a duke's household, Crusch had had many occasions to interact with Fourier in her early childhood. Given his position as a member of the royal family, Fourier could hardly have hoped for friends close to him in age — which meant Crusch might well have counted as one of his few friends.

Crusch, too, had never disliked Fourier as a person.

Fourier had frequently visited House Karsten, bringing with him unreasonable demands and nonsensical troubles just to fluster Crusch. The true motive behind his behavior had been a wish to catch the attention of a girl he was fond of — though Crusch never once noticed as much.

Still, to Crusch, Fourier was a kind-hearted, good-natured person whose character no one could bring themselves to dislike.

As a statesman, his talents were, for one connected to the Lugunican royal house, never particularly blessed. But in passion and personal virtue, he was without question a man of real substance.

His claim to the throne was a distant one, and the post he would likely have been given upon reaching adulthood would not have carried especially heavy responsibility. Even so, he had burned with enthusiasm to give his all to whatever role was entrusted to him.

That such a man now lay bedridden, teetering at death's threshold, tormented Crusch's heart terribly.

"Crusch-sama..."

Ferris, too, felt his chest ache at the sight of Crusch, her expression unchanged but her eyes carrying a color of deep grief.

In terms of the sheer length of his acquaintance with Crusch, Fourier had known her longer than Ferris had. Of course, in terms of closeness, Ferris — who followed her as her attendant — was by far the more intimate of the two, but he had watched them both closely enough to sense the feelings Fourier had long kept hidden in his heart for Crusch.

That was why Fourier was, for Ferris, someone who stirred up terribly complicated feelings.

Of course, he never let those complicated feelings color his treatment or diagnosis in any way. That was a matter of pride for him as a healer, and above all, doing so would have been nothing but a betrayal of Crusch, who trusted him enough to leave the matter in his hands.

Still, the fact that he had failed to live up to that trust, and the possibility that he might not be able to save Fourier at all, remained lodged in Ferris's heart as a heavy knot.

Together, mistress and attendant made their way through the royal castle with heavy steps.

Crusch's mind was on what lay ahead; Ferris's, on his own lack of power and on concern for his mistress.

At the very least, there was nothing more the two of them could do at the castle today. Without any word passing between them, they left the castle together and turned their steps outward, toward the second residence they kept in the royal capital.

"—Lord Bordeaux! That's not what we agreed upon!"

That voice reached them while Crusch and Ferris were on their way to the castle gate.

It was along the short stretch between the castle entrance and the main gate — in the garden lined on either side with trimmed grass and trees — that the disturbance broke out, splitting the peaceful scenery apart.

When Crusch, stopping in her tracks without meaning to, turned around, she saw two figures arguing at one corner of the garden, in a spot around the far side of the castle's outer wall.

Both were tall figures, standing face to face, one with his back turned toward Crusch. The one whose face she could see was, in fact, an acquaintance she recognized.

A bald, powerfully built old man of considerable stature. His name was Bordeaux Zergev, a member of the Council of Wise Men — one of the most important posts in the Dragon Kingdom of Lugunica.

The Council of Wise Men was a faction of high nobility that shouldered the greater part of the Lugunican government's affairs of state.

It was a post selected based on knowledge, lineage, contribution to the kingdom, and a comprehensive evaluation of one's conduct from birth to the present — and, with its representative, Miklotov McMahon, at its head, it could be said to serve as the very backbone supporting the kingdom now in its present crisis.

Bordeaux was a battle-hardened veteran who had once made a name for himself in martial prowess, and within the Council he was one of the central figures who drove debate forward with fierce opinions and an iron hand.

Known for his extreme statements and manner, Bordeaux was mocked by Ferris as being "always angry" — and back when Crusch had only just inherited headship of her house, she too had been on the receiving end of his harsh voice more times than she could count.

Perhaps it was because he was always a man of such assertive bearing.

What first drew Crusch's surprise was the uncharacteristic shadow she saw on his face as he exchanged words with someone in the garden.

"Oh-nya? That's a rare face for Lord Bordeaux to be wearing."

Ferris, beside her, murmured this, apparently having noticed the same thing as Crusch. Dipping her chin as if in agreement, Crusch hesitated over what to do.

If it would settle as nothing more than a quarrel, there was no need for Crusch to intervene and make more of it than it was. But even a minor argument was ill-timed and ill-placed here.

The gate guards, having heard the raised voice, came into the garden, but stopped short at the sight of what Crusch had seen. Then, as if deferring the decision to her, they sent their gazes her way.

With a single look, Crusch signaled the guards to hold back, and then, noticing that Bordeaux had become aware of the gathering onlookers, she caught his eye as well.

Reading the intent behind her glance — to settle the matter quickly — Bordeaux nodded with a sour expression. The old man turned back to face the person before him, wetting his lips lightly as he chose his words.

Watching that gesture through to its end, Crusch finally turned her attention to the person standing before Bordeaux. Even granting that her surprise at Bordeaux's unexpected manner had come first, it was, even by her own reckoning, an exasperatingly slow reaction.

Still holding onto a measure of self-reproach, she turned her focus to the figure before her — and Crusch understood at once why her awareness of that figure had been pushed to the back of her mind.

"—————"

It was a person clad in rags.

The rags in question turned out to be a dark navy coat, wrinkled and stained with grime in places. It hung from his tall shoulders, entirely swallowing him down past the waist. His hair, gone entirely white, combined with the first voice she had heard to suggest an advanced age. And yet, for his years, his hair retained a striking fullness, and his straight spine and broad, powerful shoulders carried none of the frailty of old age.

Conspicuous as he was, the ragged man had, until just a moment ago, somehow failed to naturally intrude on Crusch's awareness at all.

Why? Because the way he stood was, quite simply, too refined — too perfect.

A stance that was utterly natural, and yet stripped of every last wasted motion.

The instant she noticed it, Crusch found herself, for one fleeting moment, lost entirely in admiration.

As a warrior who wielded a sword in her own right, Crusch too had an ideal state she aspired toward.

The ragged man's very bearing came astonishingly close to that place Crusch aspired to reach. People called such a thing the pinnacle of martial arts — proof that this old man had, through unceasing effort and discipline, laid his fingers at last upon that ideal.

"Crusch-sama?"

"—!"

Crusch had, without realizing it, been utterly captivated by that figure's back.

At Ferris's call, she became aware of just how absorbed she had been, and while she smoothed over her outward composure, she was left faintly shaken within.

Just who in the world was that old man?

"I do feel for you. But the timing is wrong, just now. I know it's a poor thing to go back on our promise, but I ask that you understand."

Leaving Crusch's questions aside, the conversation between Bordeaux and the man resumed. Though it took the form, more than anything, of Bordeaux throwing out words meant simply to settle the matter down.

"People are gathering. You should be just as troubled as I am if this becomes public."

"Wait. Do you think words like that could possibly satisfy me? Why this sudden change of heart? After all the words you spared to assure me it was certain...!"

"It isn't your fault. It's a matter of timing. I ask that we set this aside for now. That's all I'm able to tell you at this moment. I truly am sorry."

"Absurd...!"

To the man clinging to him, Bordeaux's words rang hollow, halting.

No doubt Bordeaux's reticence stemmed from the gag order concerning the royal family's illness. Whatever promise had once existed between him and the man was now on the verge of collapsing, unfulfilled, because Bordeaux had grown too busy to see it through.

And yet he couldn't persuade the man by telling him the truth, either. One could guess at the difficult position Bordeaux found himself in — but he was too clumsy a man to simply fabricate some convenient excuse.

Or perhaps there was something in that promise itself that made Bordeaux hesitate to lie about it at all.

Which was more sincere, and which more dishonest — hiding the truth and bowing one's head, or telling a lie to win someone's understanding?

At the very least, Bordeaux did not seem to intend to deceive the man with a false account of the facts.

Bordeaux's willingness to accept the blame was admirably clean-cut, but there was no way the ragged man could be satisfied by it.

"I've heard talk of worsening relations with the Empire. If that's the reason you hesitate to let me leave the country, then at the very least, send an envoy in my stead..."

"No, no, that's not it. I beg you, please understand. Let's calm down and set up another time to talk it through properly. I can't say exactly when, not yet, but—"

"That won't make it in time! I told you already — the results come in next month. That's when we'll have our proof! ...At the very least, look at this, and reconsider."

Even in the face of Bordeaux's already-settled resolve, the ragged man pressed on desperately. As Bordeaux tried to brush past him, the man thrust something he'd pulled from within his coat toward him.

To Crusch's eyes, it looked like a thick bundle of papers bound together.

Its edges were crumpled through and through, sun-faded to an entirely different color, and faintly stained with blood and the grime of handling.

Just how precious that sheaf of paper was to the ragged man — like a lifeline he clung to — was plain to see at a glance, in the way his trembling fingertips gripped the bundle so tightly.

And yet —

"—Enough already!"

Bordeaux swung his arm roughly, batting away the bundle held out before his chest.

Whether that reaction caught the ragged man entirely off guard, or whether more force than even Bordeaux intended had gone into the blow — was likely a matter of both at once. But whatever the cause, the result was one and the same.

"—!"

The bundle of papers, knocked from the old man's fingers, scattered across the garden as though flung there.

The wind blowing through wasn't especially strong, but the paper, light as leaves, sheet by sheet, was scattered easily all the same, whirling through the air above the garden like falling white petals.

Crusch, without meaning to, half reached out a hand — and in Bordeaux's eyes, regret flickered past.

But an upheaval violent enough to swallow up all such reactions occurred in the very next instant.

"—Bordeaux!"

With a shout, the ragged man seized Bordeaux by the collar and slammed him bodily against the wall.

Struck against the back, Bordeaux's breath caught, and his massive frame, letting out a groan of pain, was lifted clear off the ground. Even pinned against the wall as he was, hoisting a body Bordeaux's size took no ordinary strength.

"—————"

Witnessing the scene unfold before her, Crusch should have called out at once to stop it.

But the overwhelming, fierce aura the ragged man let loose — an aftershock strong enough to make the very air seem to crackle and her skin prickle — left Crusch's own movement only slightly delayed.

And just like that, the ragged man fixed Bordeaux with a gaze sharp enough to run him through—

"Guards! Don't just stand there! Quick, over here!"

A high voice rang out, and time returned to a body that had been forced into stillness.

Looking back, Crusch saw Ferris, having judged the situation dire, raising a hand and calling out to the gate guards who had been watching the scene unfold in bewilderment.

Even they could hardly look away from the sight of a member of the Council of Wise Men being assaulted right before their eyes.

Three gate guards, swords in hand, rushed to the scene and surrounded the ragged man and Bordeaux.

A dangerous, bloody tension spread through the air, and the scene very nearly tipped over into open violence. But—

"—Forgive me."

Speaking those words of apology, the ragged man abruptly released Bordeaux. Still leaning against the wall, Bordeaux slid down, a hand pressed to his throat as he coughed painfully.

Looking down at him, the ragged man slowly shook his head and turned around. At that motion, the gate guards braced themselves, wariness plain on their faces, but the old man spread both hands wide, as if to show he offered no resistance.

"I'm carrying nothing. I'll come along peacefully."

The guards surrounding him showed a flicker of confusion, but soon, as if remembering their duty, flanked the old man on either side. By convention, he would now be taken straight to the guardhouse for questioning —

"...Wait. There's, there's no need to make a fuss over this. Let him go."

"Lord Bordeaux. I have no right to say this, but that won't do — it sets a poor example. Are you telling the guards who protect this castle to let a suspicious man go free?"

"Then hold him overnight at the guardhouse. After that, you may release him."

At the old man's own words, Bordeaux, frustration seeping through, gave the order. In response, the guards seemed ready to comply without protest.

Flanked by the sturdy guards and led away, the old man, in the single instant he passed by, cast a brief glance toward Crusch.

"My thanks."

That one word, spoken, likely reached no ears but Crusch's own.

What lingered in her mind was the shadow that had surfaced, in that instant, in the old man's clear blue eyes.

But Crusch was not so discourteous as to call out and prolong the exchange.

Once the old man had left the garden in that fashion, Crusch turned first to Ferris.

"Well done, acting so quickly. Sorry I didn't move in time myself."

"No, no, it was nothing much. More importantly, I'm just glad it didn't turn into something worse. By the way... Lord Bordeaux?"

Deflecting the praise, Ferris pressed a finger to his lips and turned the conversation's point toward Bordeaux.

Across from them, having recovered from the earlier shock, Bordeaux had crouched down and was in the middle of gathering up the scattered papers.

Crusch, too, gathered up several sheets that had fallen near her feet and handed them over to Bordeaux.

"...My thanks, Lord Karsten. I've shown you an unseemly sight."

"It's nothing. It's rare to see you looking so contrite, Lord Bordeaux. I feel I've been paid handsomely enough for the show. By the way, might I ask you something?"

"—————"

Bordeaux, straightening the papers he'd gathered, said nothing. Judging his silence as consent rather than refusal, Crusch tipped her chin toward the direction in which the old man had been led away.

"That man just now — I could tell he was no ordinary sort. An acquaintance of yours, Lord Bordeaux?"

"—A friend of long standing. No, I should say a comrade-in-arms. Or perhaps 'was a comrade-in-arms' would be closer to what that fellow would accept."

"It looked as though your talk had broken down. I don't mean to pry, but if you value the relationship, you'd do well to mend it soon. Though next time, you ought to choose your setting more carefully."

The place where the disturbance had occurred was what it was, and the timing was what it was.

The ragged old man's timing had been unfortunate, but it was surely Bordeaux who had handled things poorly. Precisely because she could feel the wind of regret blowing off him, Crusch found she couldn't bring herself to dislike the man before her.

"...I have no face to show him. And I don't think myself wrong for choosing loyalty over debt owed."

"I see."

And so Crusch understood, too, that Bordeaux would never heed her advice. Which was why she felt no disappointment over it. If anything, his clumsy way of being struck her as almost admirable.

Though she had no wish to say so aloud and be shouted at in his usual fashion for it.

"On your way back from visiting His Highness?"

"On my way back from seeing Prince Fourier, yes. My own Ferris's diagnosis isn't promising either."

"I imagine not. ...Just between us, Leip Barielle has been sneaking about. Knowing that petty scoundrel, he's surely plotting something. You'd best keep an eye out yourself."

"Lord Barielle, is it. I'll keep it in mind."

Nodding, she took Bordeaux's warning to heart.

It was a blatant change of subject, but pressing further would likely draw nothing more out of him.

The conversation went no further after that. Bordeaux tucked the recovered bundle of papers into his coat and hurried off, leaving the garden behind.

Left behind, Crusch and Ferris let out matching sighs.

"Ahh, that had me all on edge. Still, a meek Lord Bordeaux — now that's a rare sight, nya. If he were always like that, he'd almost be cute."

"That would cause problems of its own, I think. Between the stillness of Lord Miklotov and the motion of Lord Bordeaux, the two of them do a fine job of keeping the Council's Balance in check between them. But..."

What, in the end, had that whole disturbance been about?

No — the particulars of the disturbance were one thing, of course, but what had truly caught Crusch's interest was none other than the old man who had been led away.

If she could, she wanted to learn just who he was —

"By. The. Way! Crusch-sama, Crusch-sama. It's a little odd for me to say so myself, nya, but I think I'm the very best attendant, who understands Crusch-sama better than anyone else."

"—? That's sudden. Of course, without anyone needing to say so, you're the most precious attendant I could ever have — but what's brought this on all at once?"

"Hnnfu~. I was just thinking that today, that evaluation of yours might just take one more step forward, nya~, or something like that, nya."

Ferris stuck out his red tongue, playing the fool, but the true meaning behind his words remained frustratingly unclear. Even the wind blowing off of him gave his feelings away as if wrapped in smoke — impossible to make out clearly.

Crusch Karsten's Divine Protection of Wind Reading let her see the flow of the wind as it passed through. That wind could catch hold of something like "the waves of other people's emotions," and she faced no obstacle whatsoever in scooping up the surface feelings of virtually anyone she stood opposite.

Only rarely was there someone, like Ferris, skilled enough to hide their feelings from her.

In Ferris's case, it was because their long acquaintance had let him clearly grasp the range of wind Crusch could read. As for Miklotov of the Council of Wise Men, it was astonishing how easily he could conceal his own emotions through decades of experience and sheer conversational skill.

Crusch had come to accept it simply as a kind of negotiating skill that didn't work on true experts.

As ever, Ferris kept his feelings unreadable to Crusch. Which was why, whenever he put on such a self-important air like this, it was Crusch's habit to fold her arms and wait for the answer.

Still, she was certain that whatever he brought to her could always be trusted.

And the trust born of that bond proved its worth once again, exactly as it always did.

"I figured Crusch-sama was surely curious about that old man from earlier, nya~."

"Hm."

"So I went and prepared this."

At the sight of what Ferris, speaking with a knowing smile, held pinched between his fingertips, Crusch's eyes went wide. Then, softening, her lips eased into a smile as she looked at her attendant.

"—You really are the finest attendant there could be, at reading my wishes so well."

In the smiling Ferris's hand, one sheet of paper — one that had never made it back to Bordeaux — swayed in the wind, waiting to be taken.
